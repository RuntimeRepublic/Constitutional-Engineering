## FIRST, MEASURE THE SYSTEM WE ARE REPLACING

Kansas cannot intelligently replace property taxation without measuring the size and behavior of the economic base proposed to replace it.

Four different ideas are easy to confuse. Keep them separate:

| Concept | Plain meaning | Status in this research |
|---|---|---|
| **Current property-tax revenue** | What Kansas governments now levy/collect in property tax | About **$6.67 billion** FY 2025 (KLRD “General Property,” levied-for-collection). A related PVD/OpenGov total near **$6.82 billion** uses a different report layout. |
| **Current sales/use-tax base** | What Kansas already taxes as retail sales and use | Combined state+local sales/use about **$6.01 billion** collected FY 2025. State rate generally **6.5%**; state food rate **0%** since Jan 1, 2025 (local food tax may still apply). |
| **Estimated final-consumption base (Bill A / H.R. 25-style)** | Broader economic standard of final consumption | **Not certified.** Research uses illustrative wide scenarios only. |
| **Bill A required rate** | Rate needed after Bill A’s own authorized-spending and transition engineering | **Not yet calculable.** |

And two more:

| Concept | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Revenue-equivalent rate** | “What rate would raise about as much as today’s property tax (or today’s sales tax) on a stated base?” — a **comparison tool** |
| **Transaction distribution** | How many purchases fall near $20, $30, $100, etc. | **Authoritative Kansas public data not located.** That does **not** mean “zero small purchases.” |

### Critical firewall

**Today’s collections are not automatically Bill A’s required future collections.**  
Bill A measures Current State, identifies legitimate obligations, runs spending through accountability controls, then asks what authorized revenue is actually needed — it does **not** begin by locking “collect the same X forever.”

### Uniform rate vs transaction size

If every taxable dollar faces the **same** event-tax rate, total revenue is mainly:

**taxable dollars × rate.**

Counting how many tickets are $20 vs $100 does **not** by itself change that math. It still matters for how the tax **feels**, how hard it is to administer, and how to explain it to the public — including the practical question raised for Senator Murphy.

### Purchase-level examples (arithmetic only)

These show tax on **one** purchase. They are **not** evidence of how many Kansas transactions occur at each amount.

### Modeled comparison (not Bill A’s required rate)

Using illustrative bases from the controlled research report (MODELED SCENARIO):

- On a **central current-style ~$55B** base, raising about **$6.67B** (property-comparable) implies roughly a **~12%** tax-exclusive rate.  
- On a **broader illustrative ~$90B** final-consumption-style base, the same target implies roughly **~7%**.  
- Bill A’s planning discussion of up to **5% state** and up to **5% local** remains a **planning ceiling**, not a proven final answer. Research that shows a comparison rate above or below that ceiling is **information for Human review**, not automatic redesign.
